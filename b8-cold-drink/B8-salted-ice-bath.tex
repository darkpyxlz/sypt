A can of drink is cooled in a bath of crushed ice, water and salt. This report derives everything
that decides how cold the bath gets and how fast the drink cools, starting from conservation of
energy and of mass, the thermodynamics of salt water, and the laws of conduction, convection and
diffusion. Every equation is derived where it is first used, its symbols are named with their units,
and the conditions it needs are stated. Where physics gives only the form of a law and experiments
supply its constant (the freezing curve of strong brine, free-convection correlations, the
permeability of a heap of ice), the report says so and gives the source.

Two programs put the equations to work. \code{b8\_sim.py} treats the bath as one body at
equilibrium and answers how cold (§\ref{sec:resfloor}). \code{b8\_model.py}, behind the
interactive simulator, follows every process at its own rate and answers how fast
(§\ref{sec:ressim}). The colour code of the reporter deck is kept: \FLOOR\ for equilibrium
thermodynamics (how cold) and \RATE\ for heat transfer (how fast).

\section{Summary and status}
\label{sec:summary}

\textbf{What the physics says.} The temperature of the bath is not fixed by its recipe alone but by
its energy: the brine composition depends on how much ice has melted, and that depends on the
temperature (§\ref{sec:start}). Closing that loop with an energy balance gives four results that change
the rig. \textbf{The charge in the rig note does not reach the eutectic}: it settles near
$-17.8\dC$ with no salt left, so it has no thermostat (§\ref{sec:resrig}). \textbf{4.0\,kg of ice,
1.6\,kg of water and 1.0\,kg of salt} reaches $-21.2\dC$ and keeps 193\,g of salt as a buffer.
Reaching the eutectic is an energy condition, not a mass condition (§\ref{sec:reach}). And the drink's
heat does not reach the whole bath at once: it spreads through the ice bed no faster than the brine
can carry it (§\ref{sec:spreading}). With the can lying on its side as in the rig, the simulator
puts Can~2 at 5\,°C \textbf{222\,s} after it goes in (§\ref{sec:ressim}).

\textbf{Solid.} The enthalpy formulation and the equilibrium solve it implies (§\ref{sec:equilibrium});
the reproduction of every bath number in the earlier B8 notes once their starting temperatures are
matched (Table~\ref{tab:repro}); the result that the bath temperature cannot be found from composition
alone; the $1/\text{size}$ scaling of bath drift; the condition for reaching the eutectic; the
numerical methods, which match closed-form solutions and conserve energy to $10^{-9}$\,J.

\textbf{Computed but not measured.} The adiabatic temperature of any charge; the buffer map
(Fig.~\ref{fig:map}); the drink cooling curves for Can~2; the blank-run warming rate; how far the
drink's heat spreads; the box's heat leak.

\textbf{Uncertain.} The heat-transfer coefficients across the can, taken from a bottle study and a
SINTEF time constant rather than measured on this rig: every \RATE\ number scales with them. The
effective conductivity of the ice bed, an estimate good to a factor of two or three. The rates at
which ice melts and salt dissolves. The \FLOOR\ results move by at most 0.3\,K over the plausible
range of the textbook constants (§\ref{sec:estimates}).

\textbf{Not attempted.} Layering of the bath from top to bottom; the metastable eutectic and
hydrohalite nucleation; flow inside the drink (the COMSOL model covers that); and any dissolution law
that could explain the bench stall at $-7.5\dC$ (§\ref{sec:stall}).

\section{The system, and what is conserved}
\label{sec:conservation}

\subsection{The parts}
The rig is a can of drink standing (or lying) in a glass tank, inside a styrofoam box with a lid, in
a room at $T_{\text{room}} = 28\dC$. The tank holds the bath: liquid \emph{brine} (water with salt
dissolved in it), crushed \emph{ice}, and undissolved \emph{salt crystals} on the floor. Each part
holds heat and passes it to its neighbours:
\[
\text{room} \to \text{foam} \to \text{glass} \to \text{brine},\qquad
\text{room} \to \text{foam lid} \to \text{air} \to \text{brine},\qquad
\text{drink} \to \text{can wall} \to \text{brine} \to \text{ice}.
\]
Two quantities are conserved in every part and every exchange: energy and mass. Everything that
follows is these two conservation laws, written for each part, together with the laws that say how
fast energy and mass move between parts.

\subsection{Energy: the first law at constant pressure}
For any body that does no work other than by changing its volume against the surrounding pressure
$p$, the first law of thermodynamics reads
\begin{equation}
\mathrm dU = \delta Q - p\,\mathrm dV ,
\label{eq:firstlaw}
\end{equation}
with $U$ the internal energy (J), $\delta Q$ the heat received (J) and $V$ the volume (m$^3$). The tank
is open to the room, so $p$ stays at one atmosphere. Defining the \emph{enthalpy} $H = U + pV$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm dH = \mathrm dU + p\,\mathrm dV + V\,\mathrm dp = \delta Q \qquad (\mathrm dp = 0).
\label{eq:dH}
\end{equation}
\where Equation \eqref{eq:firstlaw} is energy conservation: what a body gains as heat it keeps as
internal energy, less the work it does pushing the atmosphere back as it expands. Adding $pV$ folds
that work into the bookkeeping, so at constant pressure every joule of heat that arrives shows up as
enthalpy. Ice shrinks by 8\,\% as it melts and brine expands slightly as it warms; with $H$ these volume
changes need no separate treatment.
\holds At constant pressure, with no stirring work counted as heat (a stirrer's power $P_{\text{stir}}$
is added to the heat it stirs into).

For a part $k$ receiving heat flows $\dot Q_{jk}$ (W) from its neighbours $j$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm dH_k}{\mathrm dt} = \sum_j \dot Q_{jk},
\qquad \dot Q_{jk} = -\dot Q_{kj}.
\label{eq:balance}
\end{equation}
Because every flow between two parts appears once with each sign, adding \eqref{eq:balance} over all
the parts inside the box cancels them: the total enthalpy changes only by the heat that crosses the
box from the room, plus the stirrer. The simulator checks exactly this after every run (the
\emph{energy bookkeeping}, §\ref{sec:numerics}).

\subsection{Mass: water and salt}
Water is present as ice (mass $m_i$) and as liquid in the brine ($m_l$); salt is present dissolved
($s$) and as crystals ($S - s$). Melting moves water from one form to the other and dissolving moves
salt, but neither is created:
\begin{equation}
m_i + m_l = W, \qquad s + (S - s) = S, \qquad w = \frac{s}{m_l + s},
\label{eq:mass}
\end{equation}
with $W$ and $S$ the totals put in (kg) and $w$ the mass fraction of salt in the brine (kg of salt per
kg of brine). A brine of $w = 0.232$ is 23.2\,wt\,\% salt.

\subsection{The enthalpy of each phase}
\label{sec:enthalpy}
Enthalpy is defined up to a constant, so a reference state is chosen: \textbf{liquid water and solid
salt at 0\,°C have zero enthalpy.} Then, for each kilogram, from \eqref{eq:dH} with $\delta Q = c\,\mathrm dT$:

\paragraph{Liquid water} Warming from 0\,°C to $T$ at constant pressure takes $c_w T$, with $c_w$ the
specific heat capacity (J\,kg$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$): $h_w(T) = c_w T$.

\paragraph{Ice} Start from the reference (liquid at 0\,°C), freeze it, which releases the latent
heat $L_0 = 333.55$\,kJ\,kg$^{-1}$, then cool the ice to $T$:
\begin{equation}
h_i(T) = -L_0 + \int_0^T c_i(T')\,\mathrm dT' \;\approx\; c_i T - L_0 .
\label{eq:hice}
\end{equation}
The heat that melts 1\,kg of ice at a temperature $T$ below zero is then the difference of the two
enthalpies at that temperature,
\begin{equation}
L(T) = h_w(T) - h_i(T) = L_0 + (c_w - c_i)\,T ,
\label{eq:kirchhoff}
\end{equation}
Kirchhoff's law. Because $c_w > c_i$, melting below 0\,°C takes \emph{less} heat: 287\,kJ\,kg$^{-1}$
at $-21.2\dC$ instead of 334. The simulator uses the IAPWS heat capacity of ice, which falls from
2097\,J\,kg$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ at 0\,°C to 1950 at $-20\dC$ ($c_i = c_{i0} + c_i' T$, so the integral in
\eqref{eq:hice} gives $c_{i0}T + \tfrac12 c_i' T^2$); \code{b8\_sim.py} holds $c_i$ constant.

\paragraph{Salt} Solid salt warms like any solid: $h_s = c_s T$, $c_s = 864$\,J\,kg$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$
(50.5\,J\,mol$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ divided by 58.44\,g\,mol$^{-1}$).

\paragraph{Dissolved salt} Dissolving NaCl in water absorbs heat: breaking the crystal lattice costs
more than hydrating the ions returns. The heat of solution is $\Delta h_s = 3.88$\,kJ\,mol$^{-1}$, or
66.4\,kJ per kg of salt (measured at 25\,°C and infinite dilution; it is held constant). A kilogram of
salt that dissolves therefore carries $\Delta h_s$ more enthalpy than when it was a crystal, and the
brine, which has to supply it, cools.

\paragraph{The whole bath} Adding the parts, with the brine (liquid water $m_l$ and dissolved salt
$s$) taken to warm with the heat capacity $c_b(w)$ of brine of its composition:
\begin{equation}
H = \underbrace{(m_l+s)\,c_b(w)\,T}_{\text{brine, sensible}}
  + \underbrace{s\,\Delta h_s}_{\text{dissolution}}
  + \underbrace{m_i\,(c_i T - L_0)}_{\text{ice}}
  + \underbrace{(S-s)\,c_s T}_{\text{undissolved salt}} .
\label{eq:H}
\end{equation}
\where Equation \eqref{eq:H} is the sum of the per-kilogram enthalpies above, each multiplied by its
mass, with every phase at the same temperature $T$. It is the single equation that connects the heat
put into the bath with its temperature and phase amounts.
\holds When the bath is at one temperature (relaxed in the simulator, which gives every ring of
brine and its ice their own temperatures, §\ref{sec:rates}), when $\Delta h_s$ does not depend on
concentration (±20\,\% moves the bath by less than 0.05\,K), and when mixing water with salt changes
the heat capacity but not the enthalpy otherwise.

The heat capacity of brine falls as salt is added. \code{b8\_sim.py} uses $c_b = 4180 - 4400\,w$;
the simulator uses a fit to Melinder (2010), $c_b = 4189 - 5794\,w + 8579\,w^2$\,J\,kg$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$,
good to $\pm 33$ between $-20$ and $+10\dC$. Table~\ref{tab:constants} lists every constant.

\section{Salt water: freezing, solubility and the eutectic}
\label{sec:phase}

\subsection{Why salt lowers the freezing point}
\label{sec:colligative}
Ice and liquid water coexist when a molecule of water has no reason to move from one to the other:
when the \emph{chemical potential} of water, the Gibbs energy per mole, is the same in both. For pure
water this happens at $T_0 = 273.15$\,K. In brine, dissolved ions dilute the water, which lowers its
chemical potential by $RT\ln a_w$, with $a_w$ the activity of water ($a_w = 1$ for pure water,
$a_w < 1$ in brine). Ice contains no salt, so its chemical potential is unchanged. Equilibrium at a
new temperature $T$ needs
\begin{equation}
\mu_{\text{ice}}(T) = \mu^{*}_{w}(T) + RT\ln a_w ,
\label{eq:mu}
\end{equation}
with $\mu^*_w$ the chemical potential of pure liquid water and $R = 8.314$\,J\,mol$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$.
Dividing by $RT$ and using the Gibbs–Helmholtz relation $\mathrm d(\mu/T)/\mathrm dT = -h_m/T^2$
for each phase ($h_m$ the molar enthalpy), the temperature derivative of $\ln a_w$ is the molar latent
heat $L_m = 6.01$\,kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ over $RT^2$; integrating from $T_0$, where $a_w = 1$,
\begin{equation}
\ln a_w = -\frac{L_m}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T_0}\right) .
\label{eq:lnaw}
\end{equation}
For a dilute, ideal solution the activity is the mole fraction of water, so
$\ln a_w = \ln(1 - x) \approx -x$, and $x \approx M_w\, i\, b$, with $b$ the molality (moles of NaCl per
kg of water), $i = 2$ the number of ions each formula unit gives, and $M_w = 0.018015$\,kg\,mol$^{-1}$.
For a small depression $\Delta T = T_0 - T$, $1/T - 1/T_0 \approx \Delta T/T_0^2$. Together:
\begin{equation}
\Delta T = K_f\, i\, b,\qquad K_f = \frac{R\,T_0^2\,M_w}{L_m} = 1.86\ \text{K\,kg\,mol}^{-1},
\qquad b = \frac{w}{M_{\text{NaCl}}(1 - w)} .
\label{eq:blagden}
\end{equation}
This is Blagden's law, the one \code{sypt code} used, and its constant follows from the latent heat
alone.
\holds Only when the brine is dilute (a few per cent), ideal (no ion–ion interactions), and fully
dissociated, and when $\Delta T \ll T_0$. None of these is true near the eutectic, where
$b = 5.2$\,mol\,kg$^{-1}$ and one particle in six is an ion.

\subsection{The measured freezing curve}
\label{sec:liquidus}
Strong brine is not ideal: water molecules bound in the ions' hydration shells are not free to
freeze, and the ions screen each other. Thermodynamics still gives \eqref{eq:lnaw} exactly; what
fails is the step $a_w \approx 1 - M_w i b$. Rather than model the activity, the model uses the
measured freezing curve, the \emph{liquidus}, in the form fitted by Bodnar (1993) to fluid-inclusion
data, with $\theta = -T$ the depression below 0\,°C:
\begin{equation}
w_L(T) = 0.0178\,\theta - 4.42\times10^{-4}\,\theta^2 + 5.572\times10^{-6}\,\theta^3,
\qquad 0 \le \theta \le 21.2 .
\label{eq:bodnar}
\end{equation}
\where Experiment, with a check from \eqref{eq:blagden}: for dilute brine \eqref{eq:blagden} gives
$w \approx M_{\text{NaCl}}\,\theta/(i K_f) = 0.0157\,\theta$, close to the leading term of the fit; the
higher terms carry the non-ideality. The ratio of Blagden's depression to the measured one is 1.106,
1.069, 1.019, 0.952 and 0.909 at 5, 10, 15, 20 and 23\,wt\,\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:liq}). The fit is explicit
in $T$, which makes the equilibrium solve fast; the simulator inverts it by Newton's method when it
needs $T$ from $w$.

\subsection{Solubility, the eutectic and the phase rule}
\label{sec:phaserule}
How many of the bath's variables are free is set by Gibbs' phase rule. At fixed pressure, a mixture
of $C$ components in $P$ phases at equilibrium has
\begin{equation}
F = C - P + 1
\label{eq:phaserule}
\end{equation}
free intensive variables: each phase has $C - 1$ independent mass fractions, the temperature adds
one, and each of the $C(P-1)$ equalities of chemical potential between phases removes one. With
water and salt ($C = 2$):
\begin{itemize}
\item brine alone ($P = 1$, $F = 2$): temperature and composition are both free;
\item brine with ice, or brine with salt crystals ($P = 2$, $F = 1$): composition follows from
temperature — the liquidus \eqref{eq:bodnar}, or the solubility line $w_{\text{sat}}(T)$;
\item brine with ice \emph{and} salt ($P = 3$, $F = 0$): nothing is free. This is the \emph{eutectic}
point, $T_E = -21.2\dC$, $w_E = w_L(T_E) = 0.2318$.
\end{itemize}
On the eutectic the bath is a thermostat: heat put in cannot change the temperature, only the amounts
of the three phases. The solubility of halite (rock salt) is $35.65 + 0.0147\,T$\,g per 100\,g of
water between 0 and 30\,°C, $w_{\text{sat}} = g/(100+g)$. Below the peritectic, $T_P = +0.1\dC$, the
stable solid is hydrohalite NaCl$\cdot$2H$_2$O, whose solubility falls from $w_P = 0.2628$ to $w_E$ at
the eutectic (taken as a straight line). Brine on the liquidus is below both solubility lines
everywhere above $T_E$, so \textbf{crystals and ice can coexist only at the eutectic}: with ice
present, salt dissolves until it is all gone or the brine reaches $w_E$.

\section{The bath at equilibrium: how cold}
\label{sec:equilibrium}

\subsection{One idea: describe the bath by its enthalpy}
Given the totals $W$ and $S$ and the enthalpy $H$, the bath has exactly one equilibrium state. The
temperature is then an output, not an input: heat that crosses the bath boundary changes $H$
\eqref{eq:balance}, and temperature, ice, brine composition and salt left on the floor are all read off
$H$. This removes the circularity of §\ref{sec:start}, and five behaviours that are usually bolted on as
separate rules come out of one function: the adiabatic mixing temperature, the drift along the
liquidus as ice melts, the eutectic thermostat, the exhaustion of the ice, and the temperature
dependence of the latent heat.

\subsection{The equilibrium solve $T_b = \mathcal T(H;\,W, S)$}
Combining the mass balances \eqref{eq:mass}, the phase rule (§\ref{sec:phaserule}) and the enthalpy
\eqref{eq:H} gives four regimes:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X l@{}}
\toprule
Regime & Phase amounts (from mass and phase equilibrium) & $T_b$ \\ \midrule
\code{liquid} & no ice, $m_l = W$;\; $s = \min\!\big(S,\; W\,r_{\text{sat}}(T)\big)$ & from \eqref{eq:H} \\

\end{center}
Here $r = w/(1-w)$ is kilograms of salt per kilogram of water, and $r_E = 0.3018$ at the eutectic.
\where \code{liquidus}: with ice present the brine sits on the freezing curve ($F = 1$); all salt is
dissolved because the liquidus lies below saturation (§\ref{sec:phaserule}); the salt balance
$s = w\,(m_l + s)$ then gives $m_l$, and the water balance gives $m_i$. \code{eutectic}: three phases,
$F = 0$, so $T$ and $w$ are fixed and only the amount of brine is unknown; \eqref{eq:H} is linear in
$m_l$ there, so the solve is a single division.

\paragraph{Uniqueness} On the \code{liquidus} branch, raising $T$ melts ice (which takes $L(T)$ per
kilogram) and warms everything, so $H(T)$ rises strictly; on the \code{liquid} branch it rises with the
heat capacity alone. $H$ is continuous where the branches meet, so $H(T)$ is strictly increasing:
\textbf{for each $H$ there is exactly one temperature}, found by bracketing on $(T_E, 200\dC)$.
\eqref{eq:H} is evaluated at both ends of the eutectic plateau with the same $w_E$ — computed from
\eqref{eq:bodnar}, not typed — or the branch and the plateau fail to meet by $10^{-3}$\,kJ and the
bracket breaks.

\subsection{The latent heat that the bath actually charges}
\label{sec:Leff}
On the liquidus with $S$ fixed, $m_l = S(1-w)/w$, so melting $\mathrm dm$ of ice changes the
composition by $\mathrm dw/\mathrm dm = -w^2/S$, and the temperature follows the liquidus,
$\mathrm dT_b = \mathrm dw / w_L'(T_b)$. The heat taken per kilogram melted is the derivative of
\eqref{eq:H}:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm dT_b}{\mathrm dm_{\text{melt}}} = \frac{w^2}{S\,\lvert w_L'(T_b)\rvert},
\qquad
L_{\text{eff}} \equiv \frac{\mathrm dH}{\mathrm dm_{\text{melt}}}
 = \underbrace{L_0 + (c_b - c_i)\,T_b}_{L(T_b)}
 + \underbrace{C_{\text{bath}}\,\frac{\mathrm dT_b}{\mathrm dm_{\text{melt}}}}_{\text{warming the bath}} .
\label{eq:Leff}
\end{equation}
\where The first term is Kirchhoff's latent heat \eqref{eq:kirchhoff} at the bath temperature, with
the meltwater joining brine of heat capacity $c_b$; the second is the heat spent warming the whole
bath, of heat capacity $C_{\text{bath}}$, along the liquidus while that kilogram melts (the dilution
raises the freezing point). \eqref{eq:H} contains both automatically; the code evaluates $L_{\text{eff}}$
by finite difference in $H$. Both factors in $\mathrm dT_b/\mathrm dm$ carry $1/S$: \textbf{double every
mass and the drift per kilogram melted halves}, the $1/\text{size}$ law of the rig note.

\subsection{Where the cold comes from}
Integrating \eqref{eq:H} between the unmixed ingredients and the mixed bath at constant $H$, the heat
that cools the bath is paid by only two terms: $L(T)\,m_{\text{melt}}$, the ice melting, and
$s\,\Delta h_s$, the salt dissolving. Their ratio can be read off any equilibrium; for the charges in the
earlier notes the salt supplies about a tenth of the cold and the ice the rest (§\ref{sec:resfloor}).

\subsection{Reaching the eutectic is an energy condition}
\label{sec:reach}
Adiabatic mixing conserves $H$. A charge reaches the thermostat only if its starting enthalpy lies
below the top of the eutectic plateau, and keeps solid salt only if it lies further below:
\begin{equation}
H_0 \;\le\; H\big(T_E;\; m_l = \min(W,\,S/r_E)\big),
\label{eq:reach}
\end{equation}
with $H_0$ the enthalpy of the ingredients at their own starting temperatures. \where The plateau's top
is the state with all the water that can be brined at $w_E$ already liquid; any more enthalpy and the
bath leaves the plateau. Free water is expensive: every kilogram of it must be cooled to $-21.2\dC$ and
salted to $w_E$, and the only payment is melting ice, which brings more water. Checking that the
salt can saturate the free water — a mass condition — is necessary but not sufficient.

\section{How heat moves}
\label{sec:heat}

\subsection{Conduction}
\label{sec:conduction}
In a solid or a still fluid heat flows down the temperature gradient. Fourier's law,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf q = -k\,\nabla T ,
\label{eq:fourier}
\end{equation}
gives the heat flux $\mathbf q$ (W\,m$^{-2}$), with $k$ the thermal conductivity (W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$).
Energy conservation \eqref{eq:balance} for a small volume, whose enthalpy changes at
$\rho c\,\partial T/\partial t$ per unit volume, turns it into the heat equation
\begin{equation}
\rho c\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \nabla\cdot(k\nabla T),\qquad
\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \alpha\,\nabla^2 T,\qquad \alpha = \frac{k}{\rho c}.
\label{eq:heateq}
\end{equation}
\where The net heat flowing into a small volume through its faces is $-\nabla\cdot\mathbf q$ per unit
volume; it raises the temperature of the mass $\rho$ per unit volume of heat capacity $c$. The
thermal diffusivity $\alpha$ (m$^2$\,s$^{-1}$) is the only combination of the three properties that
appears; in time $t$ a change of temperature diffuses a distance
\begin{equation}
\delta(t) \sim \sqrt{\alpha t},
\label{eq:difflength}
\end{equation}
because $\partial/\partial t \sim 1/t$ and $\nabla^2 \sim 1/\delta^2$ must balance (the exact solution
for a suddenly heated wall is an error function of $x/2\sqrt{\alpha t}$). For eutectic brine
$k_b = 0.54$\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$, $\rho_b = 1182$\,kg\,m$^{-3}$, $c_b = 3307$\,J\,kg$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$, so
$\alpha_b = 0.14$\,mm$^2$\,s$^{-1}$ and in three minutes conduction alone carries heat \textbf{5\,mm}; for
ice $\alpha = 1.2$\,mm$^2$\,s$^{-1}$.

\subsection{Steady conduction through layers}
\label{sec:layers}
When nothing stores heat, the same heat flow $\dot Q$ passes through every layer in a stack.
Integrating \eqref{eq:fourier} across a flat slab of thickness $\delta$ and area $A$, and across a
cylindrical shell of length $\lambda$ between radii $r_1$ and $r_2$ (where the flux falls as $1/r$
because the same $\dot Q$ crosses every circle $2\pi r\lambda$):
\begin{equation}
\dot Q = \frac{kA}{\delta}\,\Delta T,
\qquad
\dot Q = \frac{2\pi k\lambda}{\ln(r_2/r_1)}\,\Delta T .
\label{eq:slabcyl}
\end{equation}
The coefficient of $\Delta T$ is a conductance $G$ (W\,K$^{-1}$); its inverse is a resistance. Since the
temperature drops of layers in series add while the heat flow is common, \textbf{resistances in series
add}; side by side, the heat flows add, so \textbf{conductances in parallel add}. Every heat path in
the rig is built from these two rules.

\subsection{Convection and the film coefficient}
\label{sec:film}
At a wall in a moving fluid, the fluid touching the wall is at rest and passes heat only by
conduction, through a thin \emph{thermal boundary layer} of thickness $\delta_T$ in which the
temperature changes from the wall's $T_w$ to the fluid's $T_\infty$. Outside it the flow mixes the
fluid. So
\begin{equation}
q = h\,(T_w - T_\infty),\qquad h \approx \frac{k}{\delta_T},\qquad
\mathrm{Nu} = \frac{h\mathcal L}{k} \approx \frac{\mathcal L}{\delta_T} .
\label{eq:newton}
\end{equation}
\where The first equation, Newton's law of cooling, \emph{defines} the film coefficient $h$
(W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$); the physics is in how $\delta_T$ depends on the flow, and the Nusselt number
$\mathrm{Nu}$ measures it against a length $\mathcal L$ of the body.

\subsection{Free convection}
\label{sec:freeconv}
Nothing stirs the bath or the drink: fluid moves only because warm fluid is lighter. Next to a wall at
$T_w$ the fluid's density differs by $\Delta\rho = \rho\beta\,\Delta T$ ($\beta$ the thermal expansion
coefficient), so the boundary layer feels a buoyancy force $\rho g\beta\Delta T$ per unit volume. In a
viscous liquid ($\mathrm{Pr} = \nu/\alpha \gg 1$: 7–13 for the drink, 33 for cold brine) friction
balances it, $\rho g\beta\Delta T \sim \mu u/\delta_T^2$, which sets the speed $u$ of the rising (or
sinking) layer. Heat carried along the wall over a length $\mathcal L$ balances heat conducted across
the layer, $u\,\Delta T/\mathcal L \sim \alpha\,\Delta T/\delta_T^2$. Eliminating $u$:
\begin{equation}
\delta_T \sim \mathcal L\,\mathrm{Ra}^{-1/4},\qquad
\mathrm{Nu} \sim \mathrm{Ra}^{1/4},\qquad
\mathrm{Ra} = \frac{g\beta\,\Delta T\,\mathcal L^3}{\nu\alpha},
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
h \propto \Big(\frac{\Delta T}{\mathcal L}\Big)^{1/4}.
\label{eq:freeconv}
\end{equation}
\where Two balances, buoyancy against friction and convection against conduction, give the
thickness of the layer; the Rayleigh number $\mathrm{Ra}$ is the ratio of the two. Two consequences are
used directly. \textbf{(i)} The film coefficient grows as $\Delta T^{1/4}$: the drink-side film in the
simulator follows $h = h_{\text{ref}}(\Delta T/\Delta T_0)^{1/4}$ with $\Delta T_0 = 25$\,K.
\textbf{(ii)} It shrinks as $\mathcal L^{-1/4}$: a can lying on its side, for which $\mathcal L$ is the
6.6\,cm diameter rather than the 11.7\,cm height, has films $(11.66/6.6)^{1/4} = 1.15$ times stronger
from the scaling alone.
\holds For laminar boundary layers, $\mathrm{Ra} \lesssim 10^9$: here $\mathrm{Ra}$ is $10^7$–$10^8$.

The scaling fixes the exponents; the constants come from experiment. Churchill \& Chu (1975) fitted
\[
\mathrm{Nu}_{\mathcal L} = 0.68 + \frac{0.670\,\mathrm{Ra}^{1/4}}{[1 + (0.492/\mathrm{Pr})^{9/16}]^{4/9}}\ \ \text{(vertical)},\qquad
\mathrm{Nu}_D = \left\{0.60 + \frac{0.387\,\mathrm{Ra}_D^{1/6}}{[1 + (0.559/\mathrm{Pr})^{9/16}]^{8/27}}\right\}^2\ \ \text{(horizontal cylinder)}.
\]
For brine at $-15\dC$ on Can~2 these give $h = 240$–$358$\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$ upright and 280–457 lying
down, for a 5–25\,K difference: a film 1.5–2.2\,mm thick, and a ratio of 1.17–1.28 between the two
orientations (1.15–1.33 for the drink inside). The simulator takes the brine-side
$h_o = 270$\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$ and the drink-side $h_i = 400$ for an upright can (a bottle study,
§\ref{sec:lit}), and multiplies both by \code{on\_side\_factor} $= 1.2$ for a can on its side.

\subsection{Radiation}
\label{sec:radiation}
Two facing grey surfaces at absolute temperatures $T_1$ and $T_2$ exchange
$q = \varepsilon_{\text{eff}}\sigma(T_1^4 - T_2^4)$, with $\sigma = 5.67\times10^{-8}$\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-4}$ and
$1/\varepsilon_{\text{eff}} = 1/\varepsilon_1 + 1/\varepsilon_2 - 1$. Factoring
$T_1^4 - T_2^4 = (T_1 - T_2)(T_1 + T_2)(T_1^2 + T_2^2) \approx 4\bar T^3(T_1 - T_2)$ for close
temperatures,
\begin{equation}
q \approx h_{\text{rad}}\,(T_1 - T_2),\qquad h_{\text{rad}} = 4\,\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}\,\sigma\,\bar T^3 ,
\label{eq:hrad}
\end{equation}
5.0\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$ at 290\,K for $\varepsilon = 0.9$ and 3.7 at 270\,K between two such surfaces.
Radiation therefore adds to convection like a parallel film. The box's air films (§\ref{sec:box}) are
built this way: room air on the foam, $h \approx 5 + 4 = 9$; the 1\,cm gap between foam and glass,
conduction $k_{\text{air}}/\delta = 0.024/0.01 = 2.4$ plus radiation 3.5, $h \approx 6$; the still air
under the lid, warm above cold so that it cannot convect, $h \approx 4$.

\subsection{The drink as one temperature}
\label{sec:lump}
The drink is lumped: one temperature $T_d$ for the whole can. By conduction alone it would not be
uniform — the Biot number $\mathrm{Bi} = h_i\,(V/A)/k_w = 400 \times 0.0129/0.58 = 8.9$ compares the
film outside with conduction inside and is well above 0.1 — but free convection inside the can
($\mathrm{Ra} \sim 10^8$) mixes it; the lumped temperature is the volume average of the full flow
problem solved in COMSOL. Energy conservation \eqref{eq:balance} for the drink and can, of total heat
capacity $C_d = m_d c_d + m_{\text{can}} c_{\text{Al}} = 1332$\,J\,K$^{-1}$, losing heat through the can
wall of area $A$:
\begin{equation}
C_d\,\frac{\mathrm dT_d}{\mathrm dt} = -\,U A\,(T_d - T_b),
\qquad \frac{1}{U} = \frac{1}{h_i} + \frac{\delta_{\text{Al}}}{k_{\text{Al}}} + \frac{1}{h_o} .
\label{eq:drink}
\end{equation}
\where The heat flows from the drink, across the inner film, the aluminium and the outer film in
series (§\ref{sec:layers}); the wall's resistance, $10^{-4}/237 = 4\times10^{-7}$\,m$^2$K\,W$^{-1}$, is
negligible beside $1/h \approx 3\times10^{-3}$. For a constant bath temperature and constant $U$,
\eqref{eq:drink} is solved by an exponential, and with $h \propto \Delta T^{1/4}$ on both sides
($U = U_0\,(\theta/\Delta T_0)^{1/4}$, $\theta = T_d - T_b$) by separation of variables,
$\theta^{-5/4}\mathrm d\theta = -\mathrm dt/(\tau\Delta T_0^{1/4})$:
\begin{equation}
\tau = \frac{C_d}{UA},\qquad
t_{\text{target}} = \tau \ln\frac{T_{d0}-T_b}{T_{\text{target}}-T_b},\qquad
\theta(t) = \theta_0\Big(1+\frac{t}{t^*}\Big)^{-4},\;\;
t^* = 4\tau\Big(\frac{\Delta T_0}{\theta_0}\Big)^{1/4}.
\label{eq:scales}
\end{equation}
With $U_0 = 160$\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$ and $A = 310$\,cm$^2$, $\tau = 268$\,s. Both closed forms are
used to check the integrators, not as the model.

\section{Melting, dissolving and freezing: the rates}
\label{sec:rates}

\subsection{The Stefan condition at the ice surface}
\label{sec:stefan}
Ice melts at its surface, and the surface can store no heat. Applying \eqref{eq:balance} to a thin
layer straddling it: heat arrives from the brine across the brine-side film, $h_{bi}A_i(T_b - T_s)$,
and from the ice's own interior, $G_c(T_{\text{ice}} - T_s)$; it is all spent turning ice at $T_s$
into water at $T_s$:
\begin{equation}
\dot m_{\text{melt}}\,\big[h_w(T_s) - h_i(T_s)\big] = h_{bi}A_i\,(T_b - T_s) + G_c\,(T_{\text{ice}} - T_s).
\label{eq:stefan}
\end{equation}
\where Energy conservation at a moving phase boundary (the Stefan condition). $A_i$ is the wetted ice
area, $h_{bi} = 100$\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$ the brine-side film coefficient for the slow free convection
among the pieces, $T_s$ the surface temperature, $T_{\text{ice}}$ the interior temperature of the pieces
and $G_c$ the conductance from interior to surface. A negative right-hand side freezes brine onto the
ice. The interior cools as it gives heat to the surface:
$C_{\text{ice}}\,\mathrm dT_{\text{ice}}/\mathrm dt = -G_c(T_{\text{ice}} - T_s)$.

\paragraph{Area and interior conductance} The heap is $n$ cubes of side $a$, so
$a = (m_i/n\rho_i)^{1/3}$ and the wetted area is $A_i = 6na^2\,\phi_{\text{wet}}$, with
$\phi_{\text{wet}}$ the fraction of the heap below the liquid surface (§\ref{sec:level}). As the pieces
shrink, $A_i \propto m_i^{2/3}$. Heat inside a piece crosses a mean distance comparable with its
volume-to-area ratio $a/6$, so $G_c = k_i A_i/(a/6)$ (for a sphere the exact quasi-steady value is a
fifth of the radius, $0.6$ times this distance, so $G_c$ is if anything an underestimate). The
interior's time constant $C_{\text{ice}}/G_c$ is about 9\,s for 2\,cm pieces.

\subsection{The temperature of the ice surface}
\label{sec:surface}
\textbf{Bulk} (default): the surface sits at the freezing point of the brine around it,
$T_s = T_f(w)$ from \eqref{eq:bodnar}. This assumes salt reaches the surface as fast as melting
dilutes it there.

\textbf{Salt-limited}: meltwater is fresh, so it dilutes the brine at the surface to $w_s < w$, whose
freezing point is higher. Three conditions fix $T_s$, $w_s$ and the melt rate: the Stefan condition
\eqref{eq:stefan}; the liquidus at the surface, $T_s = T_f(w_s)$; and a salt balance at the surface,
\begin{equation}
\rho\,\gamma_S A_i\,(w - w_s) = \dot m_{\text{melt}}\,w_s,
\qquad \gamma_S = \frac{\gamma_T}{\mathcal R},\quad \gamma_T = \frac{h_{bi}}{\rho c_b},
\label{eq:salt3}
\end{equation}
\where Salt diffuses to the surface across the boundary layer with a transfer velocity $\gamma_S$
(m\,s$^{-1}$), the mass-transfer analogue of $h/\rho c$, and must arrive fast enough to bring the
meltwater, $\dot m$ per second, up to $w_s$. This is the three-equation ice–ocean interface of Holland \&
Jenkins (1999). Salt diffuses about a hundred times more slowly than heat ($\alpha/D_{\text{salt}} \approx
0.14\times10^{-6}/1.5\times10^{-9}$), and across a boundary layer the transfer velocities scale with
the diffusivities to the power $2/3$, so $\mathcal R = \gamma_T/\gamma_S \approx 100^{2/3} \approx 20$;
the literature gives 10–200, the default is 30.

\subsection{Salt dissolving}
\label{sec:dissolve}
A salt grain dissolves as salt diffuses away from its surface, where the brine is saturated, into the
bulk brine. Fick's law, the diffusion analogue of \eqref{eq:fourier}, across a concentration
boundary layer of thickness $\delta_c$, gives a mass flux
$j = D\rho_b(w_{\text{sat}} - w)/\delta_c$, so
\begin{equation}
\dot s = k_d\,A_s\,\rho_b\,\big(w_{\text{sat}}(T) - w\big),\qquad k_d = \frac{D}{\delta_c},
\qquad A_s = A_{s0}\Big(\frac{S - s}{S}\Big)^{2/3}.
\label{eq:dissolve}
\end{equation}
\where $k_d$ (m\,s$^{-1}$) is the mass-transfer coefficient: with $D = 1.5\times10^{-9}$\,m$^2$\,s$^{-1}$
and $\delta_c \approx 0.1$\,mm in stirred brine, $k_d \approx 1.5\times10^{-5}$ (default $10^{-5}$); an
unstirred layer ten times thicker gives $10^{-6}$. The grains are cubes of side 0.5\,mm, whose total
area $A_{s0} = 6S/(\rho_s a_s) = 5.5$\,m$^2$ for 1\,kg shrinks as their mass to the $2/3$. Above
saturation the grains grow back. While there is ice, dissolving stops at $w_E$: beyond it salt would
only come out again as hydrohalite (§\ref{sec:phaserule}). Each kilogram that dissolves takes
$\Delta h_s$ from the brine, as \eqref{eq:H} records.

\subsection{The drink freezing}
\label{sec:drinkfreeze}
The drink is mostly water with sugar ($\approx 10$\,\%) and dissolved CO$_2$. By \eqref{eq:blagden}
with $i = 1$, 10\,\% sucrose ($b = 0.33$\,mol\,kg$^{-1}$) lowers its freezing point by 0.6\,K; the CO$_2$
adds about 0.3\,K: $T_{f0} = -0.9\dC$. When a fraction $\varphi$ of the drink's water has frozen, the
solutes are all in the remaining liquid, so their molality is $1/(1-\varphi)$ times larger and, by the
same law,
\begin{equation}
T_f(\varphi) = \frac{T_{f0}}{1 - \varphi},\qquad
H_d = C_d T - \varphi\,m_{dw}\,L(T)\quad\text{on}\quad T = T_f(\varphi).
\label{eq:drinkfreeze}
\end{equation}
\where Freezing concentrates the solution, so the drink freezes over a range of temperatures, not at
one: 50\,\% of its water is frozen at $-1.8\dC$, 90\,\% at $-9\dC$. Its enthalpy is that of the liquid
less the latent heat \eqref{eq:kirchhoff} of the ice formed, with $m_{dw} = 0.89\,m_d$ the drink's water;
given $H_d$ from \eqref{eq:balance}, the model solves for $T$ and $\varphi$ together. A still drink can
supercool: it stays liquid down to $T_{f0} - \Delta T_{\text{sc}}$, and when ice nucleates the latent
heat released warms it back onto the curve.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 8
\section{Where the drink's heat goes: spreading through the bath}
\label{sec:spreading}

\subsection{Why the heat cannot reach the whole bath at once}
Once Can~2 is in, about 217\,W leaves it ($T_d = 26.4\dC$, with the brine next to it already at
$-17.9\dC$). A perfectly mixed bath would share that at once among 2.55\,kg of ice and 3.97\,kg of
brine. But by \eqref{eq:difflength} conduction carries heat only 5\,mm through still brine in the three
minutes the drink takes to cool. The brine next to the can warms, its ice melts first, and the drink
faces warmer brine than the bulk.

\subsection{How far the heat gets before the ice takes it}
\label{sec:penetration}
Brine warmer than the ice surface loses heat to the ice at $h_{bi}a_v(T - T_s)$ per unit volume, with
$a_v$ the wetted ice area per unit volume of bath (135\,m$^{-1}$ at drink-in). In a steady state near
the can, heat conducted outwards at an effective conductivity $k_{\text{eff}}$ is absorbed by the ice
on the way. Energy conservation \eqref{eq:heateq} with this sink, in one dimension $x$ out from the can:
\begin{equation}
k_{\text{eff}}\,\frac{\mathrm d^2T}{\mathrm dx^2} = h_{bi}a_v\,(T - T_s)
\;\Longrightarrow\;
T - T_s \propto e^{-x/\ell},\qquad
\ell = \sqrt{\frac{k_{\text{eff}}}{h_{bi}\,a_v}} = 0.6\ \text{cm (still brine)},\ 2.8\ \text{cm}\ (k_{\text{eff}} = 20\,k_b).
\label{eq:pen}
\end{equation}
\where Conduction in, absorption by melting out: the temperature excess decays exponentially over the
\emph{penetration length} $\ell$. Most of the drink's heat is absorbed within a few centimetres of the
can.

\subsection{The ice bed conducts and convects}
\label{sec:convfactor}
Still brine is only a lower bound for $k_{\text{eff}}$. Two things raise it. First, ice conducts four
times better than brine. A bed of porosity $\varepsilon = 0.4$ conducts between the series and
parallel limits of its two phases,
\begin{equation}
\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{k_b} + \frac{1-\varepsilon}{k_i}\Big)^{-1} \le k_{\text{bed}} \le \varepsilon k_b + (1-\varepsilon)k_i,
\label{eq:bedk}
\end{equation}
0.98–1.54\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$, or 2–3 times $k_b$: the two limits are layers of brine and ice across and
along the heat flow, in series and in parallel (§\ref{sec:layers}). Second, the brine moves through the bed. Flow through a porous bed obeys
Darcy's law, speed proportional to the driving pressure gradient, here buoyancy:
$u \sim K g\beta\Delta T/\nu$, with $K$ the permeability (m$^2$). Modelling the gaps as channels of
width $\propto \varepsilon a/(1-\varepsilon)$ with Poiseuille flow gives the Kozeny–Carman form
$K = a^2\varepsilon^3/180(1-\varepsilon)^2 = 4\times10^{-7}$\,m$^2$ (the 180 is empirical). Repeating the
boundary-layer balance of §\ref{sec:freeconv} with Darcy's law in place of viscous friction,
$u\,\Delta T/\mathcal L \sim \alpha_m\Delta T/\delta^2$ with $u$ now independent of $\delta$, gives
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Nu} \sim \mathrm{Ra}_D^{1/2},\qquad \mathrm{Ra}_D = \frac{g\beta\,\Delta T\,K\,\mathcal L}{\nu\,\alpha_m},
\label{eq:darcy}
\end{equation}
with mean $\mathrm{Nu} = 0.89\,\mathrm{Ra}_D^{1/2}$ for a heated vertical wall (Cheng \& Minkowycz 1977)
and $\alpha_m$ the bed's diffusivity. For a 14.5\,cm bed and $\Delta T = 1$–6\,K, $\mathrm{Ra}_D \approx
10^2$–$10^3$ and $\mathrm{Nu} \approx 9$–27. Together, the \emph{convection factor}
$f = k_{\text{eff}}/k_b \approx (k_{\text{bed}}/k_b)\,\mathrm{Nu} \approx 20$–60. The estimate is rough —
the gaps (about 1\,cm) are not small next to the 2\,mm film on the can, and warm brine rising past the
can spreads along the top of the bath — so $f$ is an input; the default 20 is the low end. $f = 1$ is
still brine, $f = \infty$ a perfectly mixed bath.

\subsection{Rings round the can}
\label{sec:rings}
The bath is divided into $N$ rings (default 8): shells round the can's axis from its wall,
$r_c = 3.30$\,cm, out to $R = \sqrt{A_f/\pi} = 11.45$\,cm, the radius of a circle with the floor area
$A_f$ of the tank. Each ring is a finite volume obeying \eqref{eq:balance}, with its own brine
temperature $T_j$, ice, ice-interior temperature and crystals; the salt fraction $w$ is shared, since
the brine that carries heat also mixes salt. The edges are spaced evenly in $\ln r$,
\begin{equation}
r_j = r_c\,(R/r_c)^{j/N},\qquad j = 0,\dots,N,
\label{eq:rings}
\end{equation}
so the rings are thin by the can (0.56\,cm) and wide at the walls (1.65\,cm), where little happens.
Ring $j$ holds the share $\psi_j = (r_{j+1}^2 - r_j^2)/(R^2 - r_c^2)$ of the brine, ice and crystals. The
drink heats only the inner ring; the glass, the lid, the gaps in the box and the stirrer heat only the
outer one:
\begin{align}
C_j\,\dot T_j &= G\,(T_{j-1} - T_j) + G\,(T_{j+1} - T_j) - Q_{i,j} - \Delta h_s\,\dot s_j \notag\\
  &\quad + \delta_{j0}\,UA\,(T_d - T_0)
  + \delta_{j,N-1}\,(Q_{\text{glass}} + Q_{\text{lid}} + Q_{\text{gaps}} + P_{\text{stir}}),
  \label{eq:ring}\\
G &= \frac{2\pi\,k_{\text{eff}}\,\lambda}{\ln(r_{j+1}/r_j)} = \frac{2\pi N k_{\text{eff}}\,\lambda}{\ln(R/r_c)},
  \qquad k_{\text{eff}} = f\,k_b(w).
  \label{eq:G}
\end{align}
\where \eqref{eq:ring} is \eqref{eq:balance} for ring $j$, of heat capacity $C_j$: heat conducted in from
each neighbour, heat lost to its ice ($Q_{i,j}$, \eqref{eq:stefan}) and to dissolving its crystals, and the
drink or the room where they touch it ($\delta$ is 1 for the named ring and 0 otherwise). $G$ is the
cylindrical-shell conductance \eqref{eq:slabcyl} between the middles of neighbouring rings, which are
one step of $\ln r$ apart; with even spacing in $\ln r$ it is the same for every pair. $\lambda$ is the
length of the shells: the can's own length (11.66\,cm) for a can on its side, the depth of brine for an
upright one. At drink-in, $G = 51$\,W\,K$^{-1}$; from can to walls the bath adds
$\ln(R/r_c)/(2\pi k_{\text{eff}}\lambda) = 0.16$\,K\,W$^{-1}$ in series with the can's own
$1/UA = 0.20$\,K\,W$^{-1}$, although by \eqref{eq:pen} most of the heat is absorbed before the walls.
The rings must resolve $\ell$: 8 rings are within 4\,\% of 50 at the default, but still brine needs 50
(§\ref{sec:ressim}).

\section{The liquid level and the areas that follow it}
\label{sec:level}

Every heat path into or out of the brine crosses a wetted surface, and most of those surfaces depend
on how high the liquid stands. At every step the level is found from the volumes, and each area from
the level.

\subsection{Volumes}
The liquid occupies $V_l = (m_l + s)/\rho_b(w) + (S-s)/\rho_s$: brine of density
$\rho_b = 1000 + 786\,w$\,kg\,m$^{-3}$ (Melinder), and the crystals on the floor, $\rho_s = 2165$. Dissolving
a kilogram of salt changes the brine volume by $[\rho_b - 786(1-w)]/\rho_b^2 = 0.41$\,L, less than the
0.46\,L of the crystal, and makes the brine denser.

\subsection{A heap resting on the floor, or floating}
The crushed ice is a heap of porosity $\varepsilon$: a fraction $1-\varepsilon$ of its volume is ice. Its
thickness is $t_h = m_i/[\rho_i(1-\varepsilon)A_f]$, 17.7\,cm for 4\,kg in a 412\,cm$^2$ tank. While it rests
on the floor the liquid has only the gaps; once there is enough of it, the heap floats. By Archimedes,
a floating heap displaces its own weight of brine, $V_{\text{sub}} = m_i/\rho_b$, wherever its ice is.
The level $Z$ is where the volume below it, $A_f Z$, holds the liquid and the submerged ice:
\begin{equation}
Z = \frac{V_l}{\varepsilon A_f}\ \ \text{(resting)},\qquad
Z = \frac{V_l + m_i/\rho_b}{A_f}\ \ \text{(floating)},
\label{eq:level}
\end{equation}
and the part of the can under the surface adds its own volume over $A_f$. \where Volume conservation
and Archimedes. The heap lifts off when the liquid fills its gaps to the depth at which it would float,
$V_l = \varepsilon A_f\,m_i/[\rho_b(1-\varepsilon)A_f]$; at that point both expressions give the same $Z$, so
the level is continuous. The wetted fraction of the ice is $Z/t_h$ while resting and $\rho_i/\rho_b$ while
floating, again continuous.

With the defaults the level rises from 12.5 to 13.7\,cm in the first half-minute: the heap still rests
on the floor, and water melting off the ice fills its gaps, raising the level 2.5 times faster per litre
than open liquid would. The brine then becomes dense enough to float the heap, and as the rest of the
salt dissolves, the denser brine lifts the floating ice, so the level eases back to 13.4\,cm. That is
Archimedes for a bath mixed all at once at $t = 0$; a real bath, made up over a minute, rises over that
minute. As floating ice melts later, its meltwater takes a little more room than the brine it
displaced, and the level creeps up about 2\,mm.

\subsection{The can in the bath}
\label{sec:canlevel}
In the rig the can lies on its side, along the tank. The part of its circular end below a depth $y$
measured from its lowest point is a circular segment, a sector of the circle less the triangle over the
chord:
\begin{equation}
S(y) = r_c^2\arccos\!\Big(\frac{r_c - y}{r_c}\Big) - (r_c - y)\sqrt{2r_c y - y^2},
\qquad V_{\text{under}}(y) = H_c\,S(y),
\label{eq:segment}
\end{equation}
with $H_c = 11.66$\,cm the can's length ($V_{\text{under}} = \pi r_c^2 y$ for an upright can). With the
can's lowest point at $z_c$, the depth under the surface $y$ satisfies $y = Z_0 - z_c + V_{\text{under}}(y)/A_f$,
because the displaced volume raises the level ($Z_0$ is the level without the can); this has one root,
since $H_c$ times the chord is less than $A_f$. A can held on its holder comes to rest at $z_c = 1$\,cm;
a free can floats where $\rho_b V_{\text{under}} = m_{\text{can}}$ (68\,\% of its height under, lying
down). It is pushed down from the surface over 10\,s, so its 0.40\,L raises the level by
$0.40/412 = 0.97$\,cm smoothly, within the first 5\,s: lying down it is only 6.6\,cm high.

\subsection{The can's heat-passing area}
\label{sec:canarea}
Heat crosses the can wall only where there is liquid on both sides: brine outside, up to the brine's
surface, and drink inside, up to the drink's own surface. Above the drink is gas, a headspace of
$h_s = 1.2$\,cm measured upright, which passes little heat. Lying down, the same 41\,cm$^3$ of gas makes a
strip of depth $h_g$ along the top, found from $H_c\,S(h_g) = \pi r_c^2 h_s$: $h_g = 1.05$\,cm. The can
then passes heat through both ends and the curved wall below the strip:
\begin{equation}
A_c = 2\,S(y) + 2 r_c \arccos\!\Big(\frac{r_c - y}{r_c}\Big) H_c,\qquad y = \min(d,\ 2r_c - h_g),
\label{eq:canarea}
\end{equation}
with $d$ the depth of can under the brine. \where The ends contribute two segments of depth $y$ and
the curved wall an arc of length $2r_c\arccos[(r_c - y)/r_c]$ along the whole length. Submerged, Can~2
passes heat through 240\,cm$^2$ of its 310\,cm$^2$. Standing upright it would be the bottom and the side up
to the drink, $\pi r_c^2 + 2\pi r_c\min(d, H_c - h_s) = 251$\,cm$^2$. Either way the area grows smoothly
with the level.

\paragraph{Glass} The brine wets the floor and the walls up to $Z$: $A_g = A_f + 2(W_t + \ell_t)Z$, with
$W_t$ and $\ell_t$ the tank's width and length; it passes heat across the brine film and half the glass
thickness, $1/U_g = 1/h_{bg} + \delta_g/2k_g$, with $h_{bg} = 270$\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 10
\section{The box: heat from the room}
\label{sec:box}

The room is 48\,K warmer than the bath, so heat leaks in through the styrofoam all the time.

\subsection{A layer of foam that stores heat}
Each path through the box is a series of layers (§\ref{sec:layers}): the room's air film, the foam,
and whatever is inside. The foam also holds heat, $C_f = \rho_f c_f A t$ (280\,J\,K$^{-1}$ for the whole
box). Lumping that heat at the middle of the foam splits the path in two:
\begin{equation}
G_{\text{out}} = \frac{A}{1/h_{\text{out}} + t/2k_f},\quad
G_{\text{in}} = \frac{A}{t/2k_f + 1/h_{\text{in}}},\quad
C_f\frac{\mathrm dT_f}{\mathrm dt} = G_{\text{out}}(T_{\text{room}} - T_f) - G_{\text{in}}(T_f - T_{\text{inside}}).
\label{eq:foam}
\end{equation}
\where \eqref{eq:balance} for the foam node, with each half of the foam ($t/2$ of conductivity
$k_f = 0.035$\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$) in series with its film. In steady state the node passes on what it
receives and the two halves add back to the full series resistance, $UA = A/(1/h_{\text{out}} + t/k_f +
1/h_{\text{in}})$. Areas are taken at the middle of the foam.

\subsection{Four paths}
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l r@{}}
\toprule
Path & Leak \\ \midrule
foam walls $\to$ air gap $\to$ tank glass $\to$ brine & 0.20\,W\,K$^{-1}$ \\
foam floor $\to$ tank glass $\to$ brine (the tank stands on the foam: no air gap) & 0.07\,W\,K$^{-1}$ \\
foam lid $\to$ still air $\to$ brine & 0.05\,W\,K$^{-1}$ \\
gaps $\to$ brine: lid seam, the lid's thin lip, holes for probe leads & 0.02\,W\,K$^{-1}$ \\ \midrule
all of them & 0.34\,W\,K$^{-1}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The gaps bypass the foam. Air in the box is cold and dense, so through a slot of height $\delta_s$ and
width $W_s$ in the lid seam it flows out at the bottom of the slot and room air flows in at the top. The pressure
difference driving it grows linearly with height from the middle of the slot,
$\Delta p = \Delta\rho\,g\,z$, and Bernoulli's equation turns it into a speed
$u = \sqrt{2\Delta p/\rho} = \sqrt{2g'z}$, with $g' = g\Delta T/T$; integrating over the half-height that
flows each way,
\begin{equation}
Q_{\text{air}} = \frac{C_D}{3}\,W_s\,\delta_s\sqrt{g'\delta_s},
\label{eq:exchange}
\end{equation}
\where Hydrostatics and Bernoulli, with a discharge coefficient $C_D \approx 0.6$ for the contraction
of the jet (Linden 1999). For a 1\,mm seam round the lid ($W_s = 1.1$\,m) it carries
$\rho c_p Q_{\text{air}}\Delta T \approx 0.2$\,W, 0.005–0.01\,W\,K$^{-1}$ of leak; a 5\,mm gap carries
$5^{3/2} = 11$ times as much. With the lid's thin lip ($\approx 0.01$\,W\,K$^{-1}$) the gaps are estimated at 0.02\,W\,K$^{-1}$.

\subsection{Measuring the leak: the box test}
\label{sec:boxtest}
Fill the tank with cold water only, close the box, and log the water. After the first half-hour, while
the room-temperature glass gives up its heat, the water and glass (heat capacity $C$) warm only by the
leak, $C\,\mathrm dT/\mathrm dt = (UA)_{\text{box}}(T_{\text{room}} - T)$, whose solution approaches the
room exponentially. Taking logarithms at two times,
\begin{equation}
(UA)_{\text{box}} = \frac{C}{t_2 - t_1}\,\ln\frac{T_{\text{room}} - T(t_1)}{T_{\text{room}} - T(t_2)},
\qquad C = m_w c_w + C_{\text{glass}} = m_w \times 4190 + 2020\ \text{J\,K}^{-1}.
\label{eq:boxtest}
\end{equation}
The model predicts 6.7\,°C at 30\,min and 9.4\,°C at 3\,h for 5\,kg of water from 4\,°C, and
\eqref{eq:boxtest} applied to its own curve gives back its leak to 1\,\%. \code{box\_test.toml} sets the
run up; a measured value goes into \code{[box] measured\_UA}, which scales every path to add up to it.

\section{Putting it together: two models and how they are solved}
\label{sec:numerics}

\subsection{The equilibrium model, \code{b8\_sim.py}}
The bath is one body at equilibrium, described by its enthalpy $H_b$; the drink is a lump. Energy
conservation \eqref{eq:balance} for each, with the drink's loss \eqref{eq:drink} as the bath's gain:
\begin{align}
C_d\,\dot T_d &= -\,U A\,(T_d - T_b),
\notag\\
\dot H_b &= \underbrace{U A\,(T_d - T_b)}_{\text{from the drink}}
  + \underbrace{(UA)_{\text{box}}\,(T_{\text{room}} - T_b)}_{\text{leak}}
  + \underbrace{P_{\text{stir}}}_{\text{stirrer}},
\qquad T_b = \mathcal T(H_b),
\label{eq:bath}
\end{align}
with $U = U_0$ (Newton) or $U = U_0\,(|T_d-T_b|/\Delta T_0)^{1/4}$ (free convection). Salt added during
a run enters as $\Delta S$ with $\Delta H = \Delta S\,c_s T_{\text{room}}$. The pair is integrated by
Heun's method (a predictor step and a trapezoidal corrector, second-order accurate) at
$\Delta t = 0.25$\,s; against the closed forms \eqref{eq:scales} it reproduces the time to 5\,°C to
0.01\,s.

\subsection{The time-dependent model, \code{b8\_model.py}}
The simulator follows, as functions of time: the drink's enthalpy (and whether ice has nucleated in
it); the liquid water and dissolved salt; for each ring, its brine enthalpy, its crystals, its ice and the
ice's interior temperature; the glass temperature; and the temperatures of the foam walls and lid. Each
obeys \eqref{eq:balance} or a mass balance, with the flows of §\ref{sec:heat}–\ref{sec:box}: the drink
\eqref{eq:drink} and \eqref{eq:drinkfreeze}; the rings \eqref{eq:ring}; the ice \eqref{eq:stefan}; the
crystals \eqref{eq:dissolve}; the glass $C_g\dot T_g = Q_{\text{walls}} - U_gA_g(T_g - T_b)$; the foam
\eqref{eq:foam}. After each melt or dissolution the brine is shared out again between the rings in
proportion to their volume, carrying its heat with it.

\paragraph{Time stepping} Each quantity is stepped forward by Euler's method,
$y_{n+1} = y_n + \Delta t\,\dot y(y_n)$, with $\Delta t = 0.5$\,s. Euler's method is stable for a process
of time constant $\tau$ only if $\Delta t < 2\tau$, and accurate if $\Delta t \ll \tau$. The fastest
processes are the interior of the ice pieces (9\,s), the brine next to the can (60\,s) and the glass
(75\,s); the program checks these at the start and warns if $\Delta t$ is too long. The spreading
between rings can be much faster when the inner ring is thin, so it alone is stepped backward,
\[
C_j\big(T_j^{n+1} - T_j^{*}\big) = G\,\Delta t\,\big(T_{j-1}^{n+1} - 2T_j^{n+1} + T_{j+1}^{n+1}\big),
\]
a tridiagonal system solved exactly in one sweep: stable for any $G$, and since each flux appears with
both signs, it conserves energy exactly. With $f = \infty$ all rings take one temperature, which
reproduces the first version of the simulation (\code{inputs\_original.toml}) to $10^{-13}$\,K.

\paragraph{Energy bookkeeping} After every run the change in total enthalpy is compared with the heat
let in from the room and the stirrer \eqref{eq:balance}. It closes to $10^{-9}$\,J of the 43\,kJ let in.
That proves the bookkeeping, not the physics: a wrong heat-transfer coefficient conserves energy just
as well. Water and salt are conserved to $10^{-9}$\,kg.

meltwater, which dilutes it. For the five pre-dissolved recipes in the 10 Sep notes the model
gives $-12.0$ to $-12.5\dC$ (notes: $-12.1$ to $-12.6$); \textbf{saturating the brine at 25\,°C

By \eqref{eq:reach}, for 5.6\,kg of water in total, 1.0\,kg of salt and water at 4\,°C, the ice fraction
must exceed \textbf{0.612} to reach $T_E$ and \textbf{0.691} to keep 150\,g of salt solid (ice at 0\,°C);

(equation bank: $+17$). Rebuilding the rig note's four baths on their own liquidus and melting 150\,g
gives $+5.91$, $+1.85$, $+0.97$, $+0.49$\,K, its table to two decimals, the $1/\text{size}$ law. On the
plateau, forming 1\,kg of eutectic brine absorbs \textbf{241\,kJ} and consumes 0.960\,g of solid salt per
kJ. The rig note's 272\,kJ\,kg$^{-1}$ used $L_0$ at 0\,°C; at $-21.2\dC$ the latent heat is lower
\eqref{eq:kirchhoff}, so the buffer is used up \textbf{13\,\% faster} than that note assumed.

pinned. Beyond that, more salt buys only hold time.

\textbf{The rig-note charge has no thermostat.} It saturates its free water, which is what the
rig note checked, but adiabatically it lands at $-17.8\dC$ with every gram of salt dissolved,
and even with $-18\dC$ freezer ice only at $-19.5\dC$. The “$\geq 150$\,g stays solid” line was
a mass balance; the energy balance \eqref{eq:reach} says otherwise. \textbf{Use 4.0\,kg ice +
1.6\,kg water + 1.0\,kg salt}: $-21.2\dC$ with 193\,g solid, an equilibrium volume of about
6.2\,L, plus 0.40\,L for the can, inside the 6.8\,L to the 16.5\,cm line. Add the ice in two lots;
4\,kg of crushed ice heaps above the brim until the first lot melts back. One drink plus 25\,min
of leak uses 95\,g of the 193\,g, so top up the salt between runs and watch for crystals on the
floor.

$\dot Q = L\,\dot T_b/(\mathrm dT_b/\mathrm dm)$ recovers the true 17.7\,W leak of the unbuffered
charge as 15.5\,W with $L_f$ ($-13\,\%$) and 17.6\,W with $L_{\text{eff}}$ \eqref{eq:Leff} ($-1\,\%$); for
the rig-note charge, 16.9 against 21.1\,W ($-20\,\%$) and 20.8\,W ($-1\,\%$). Fit the slope over the
first ten minutes: $\mathrm dT_b/\mathrm dm$ itself falls as the bath dilutes, so a one-hour
average biases the answer by another 5\,\%. And the buffered bath cannot be used for this at
all — it reads zero slope for 2.5\,h — which is why the rig note asked for the blank to be run
unbuffered.

\textbf{Rings.} The rings need to resolve $\ell$ \eqref{eq:pen}. At the default, 4, 8, 16, 32 and
50 rings give 214, 222, 226.5, 229 and 230\,s, so 8 are within 4\,\%. In still brine $\ell$ is

474\,s; use 50 for $f$ below about 5. Between $f = 20$ and 60 the time to 5\,°C moves only from 222 to
202.5\,s, because the can's own films then dominate; below 10 it rises quickly.

\textbf{What matters most.} Over plausible ranges the inputs that move the time to 5\,°C most are
$h_o$ ($\div 3$ to $\times 3$: 442.5 to 147.5\,s), a measured $U$ (120–200\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$: 339.5 to 218.5\,s),
\code{on\_side\_factor} (1.0–1.4: 259 to 195.5\,s), the drink's mass or heat capacity ($\pm 20$\,\%: 268

246\,s) and the headspace (0.6–2.0\,cm: 210 to 237\,s). The \FLOOR\ numbers hardly move. A fit of $U$ from a
drink run (E3) depends on where the brine probe is: \textbf{within about 5\,mm of the can it measures the
can alone}; out in the bath it measures the can and the spreading together, and should be used with
$f = \infty$. Two brine probes, one at the can and one at least 5\,cm away, measure $f$: the default
predicts a peak difference of about 6.5\,K, still brine 16\,K, a stirred bath 3\,K.

\textbf{The box.} A box leaking anything from 0.25 to 1.0\,W\,K$^{-1}$ gives 222 to 223\,s: next to the
can's 4.9\,W\,K$^{-1}$ the leak hardly touches the drink. It shows in the bath instead: by the end of the
50-minute run the default bath is at $-19.3\dC$ with 2.09\,kg of ice left, against $-17.5\dC$ and
1.93\,kg at 1.0\,W\,K$^{-1}$.

\textbf{Still left out.} Each ring has one temperature from floor to surface, so the vertical layering of
§\ref{sec:stall} is not in it. Convection is a fixed multiple of $k_b$, not a flow driven by the
temperatures it carries. The tank is taken as a cylinder of the same floor area, and the drink as one
temperature. Crushed ice pressed against the can or the glass would conduct directly; only brine is
counted.

\textbf{The simulator.} Every number it uses is in \code{inputs.toml}, with its unit and a tag saying
where it comes from; a short file with only the lines that change overrides it. \code{b8\_app.py} and
the web page (\url{https://darkpyxlz.github.io/sypt/}, the same Python files run in the browser) put
the main inputs on sliders, each with a box for typing an exact value, and draw the tank to scale with
the brine ring by ring. A run takes about 0.5\,s in Python and 1\,s in the browser.

\section{Open problem: why did the one-shot bath stall?}
\label{sec:stall}

On 10 Sep, 75\,g ice + 25\,g water + 18\,g salt added at once stalled at $-7.5\dC$, against an
equilibrium of $-21.2\dC$. The notes attribute this to a crust on the floor in cold,
near-saturated brine. The simulation can test the simplest version of that idea, a well-mixed
bath with the dissolution law \eqref{eq:dissolve} written as
\begin{equation}
\dot s = k\,(S-s)^{2/3}\,S^{1/3}\,\frac{w_{\text{sat}}(T_b) - w}{w_{\text{sat}}(T_b)},
\label{eq:kin}
\end{equation}
\where \eqref{eq:dissolve} with the grain area proportional to the salt put in, $A_{s0} \propto S$:
$k = k_d\rho_b w_{\text{sat}}A_{s0}/S$ (s$^{-1}$) gathers the constants. Whatever $k$ is,
\textbf{this law makes the one-shot bath faster than the staged one} (Fig.~\ref{fig:kin}): at 10\,min,
$-12.3$ against $-9.0\dC$ at $k = 2\times10^{-3}$\,s$^{-1}$, because the one-shot bath has more crystal
area exposed from the start. The bulk brine at $-7.5\dC$ is only 11\,wt\,\% against a saturation of
about 25\,wt\,\%, so in a mixed bath the drive never vanishes and nothing can stall.

So the stall is evidence that the bath was not well mixed. Dense saturated brine around the
crust is gravitationally stable and stays on the floor; the crust then dissolves into a layer
that is already saturated, and the bulk only receives salt as fast as that layer is exchanged.
Stirring sets the exchange rate, and its absence sets it near zero. If this is right, the
result is not a dissolution constant but a mixing one, and it predicts something cheap to check:
\textbf{a probe on the floor of a stalled one-shot bath should read colder than the bulk}, near
the liquidus of saturated brine, and stirring should release the stall in seconds. A probe
reading the same as the bulk kills the stratification picture and puts the explanation back in
surface kinetics.

\section{Experimental programme}
\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{E1 · Charge check, one afternoon} Mix the rig-note and buffered charges side by side
in the tank, logging $T_b$ until it settles. Predicted $-17.8$ and $-21.2\dC$, with crystals on
the floor of the second only. IV: charge recipe. DV: settled $T_b$, presence of solid salt.
Controls: starting temperatures of all three components, recorded.

\paragraph{E2 · The box's leak} Unbuffered charge, lid on, no drink, one hour. Fit
$\dot T_b$ over the first ten minutes; invert with $L_{\text{eff}}$ \eqref{eq:Leff}. Predicted
0.62\,K\,h$^{-1}$ for 0.46\,W\,K$^{-1}$ with the stirrer off; the stirrer's power adds to the leak, so
measure it once with the stirrer off as well. DV: $(UA)_{\text{box}}$. A simpler measurement of the
same number, with no phase change to invert, is the box test of §\ref{sec:boxtest}: cold water only,
three hours, \eqref{eq:boxtest}.

\paragraph{E3 · Drink runs} Can~2 from room temperature in the buffered and unbuffered charges, lying
on its side as in the rig. DV: $T_d(t)$, $T_b(t)$. Fit both Newton and the $\Delta T^{1/4}$ law to
each run, compare residuals, and report $U$ rather than $\tau$. Log the brine with two probes, one
within 5\,mm of the can and one at least 5\,cm away: fitted against the first, $U$ is the can's alone;
the difference between them measures the convection factor $f$ (§\ref{sec:convfactor}), predicted to
peak near 6.5\,K. Feed the measured $T_b(t)$ of the unbuffered run into \eqref{eq:drink} as a check of
the bath model independent of the drink model.

\paragraph{E4 · Stall test} 150\,g ice + 60\,g water + 38\,g salt at once, unstirred, probes at
the floor and mid-depth. Then repeat staged. DV: floor minus bulk temperature; time for the stall
to clear after stirring starts.

\paragraph{E5 · The original charge} 1\,kg ice, 2\,kg water at 25\,°C, 0.5\,kg salt. Predicted
$-10.5\dC$ with about 50\,g of ice left, so the ice should be visibly almost gone. A direct,
memorable test of why \code{temp()} could not work.

Suggested order: E1, E2, E3, E4, E5. E1 decides whether E3 has a thermostat.

\section{Estimates justifying the approximations}
\label{sec:estimates}

\subsection{One bath temperature}
\code{b8\_sim.py} gives the bath a single $T_b$; the simulator splits it into rings round the can
(§\ref{sec:rings}), with the brine by the can 6.5\,K above the bulk at the peak. Neither resolves height:
§\ref{sec:stall} argues that layering is what stalls an unstirred one-shot bath. With the stirrer on and
probes at least a few centimetres from the can, a single $T_b$ is the right first model for the \FLOOR.

\subsection{The drink as a lump}
The drink's Biot number is about 9 (§\ref{sec:lump}), so it is not a lump by conduction; internal
convection rescues it by a factor of about 9. The lumped model is the volume average of the PDE solved
in COMSOL, which is the justification, and the 4\,°C density maximum of water is outside it.

\subsection{The eutectic solid}
At $T_E$ the equilibrium solid is ice + hydrohalite NaCl$\cdot$2H$_2$O, not ice + halite. The
formation enthalpy is $-0.73$\,kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ (Drebushchak 2017), 12.5\,kJ per kg of salt, against
about 1040\,kJ per kg of salt consumed on the plateau: 1.2\,\%. The water locked into hydrohalite
matters more for the buffer accounting, but hydrohalite nucleates reluctantly, so on the time
scale of one run the halite model is the better description.

\subsection{Assumed constants}
Changing each placeholder over a plausible range (\code{run\_checks()} section m) moves the unbuffered
$T_b$ by at most 0.11\,K, the rig-note $T_b$ by at most 0.29\,K, and the buffer by at most 16\,g of its
193\,g. No \FLOOR\ conclusion depends on them.

\subsection{Numerics}
Against a constant bath the equilibrium model's integrator reproduces $t_{5\,°\text C}$ from
\eqref{eq:scales} to 0.01\,s (169.07\,s both) and the power-law closed form to 0.01\,s (154.61\,s both).
Quartering the step changes $t_{5\,°\text C}$ by $10^{-3}$\,s. The heat released by the drink and the time
integral of the flux agree to $10^{-3}$\,kJ in 65.44\,kJ. The simulator closes its energy bookkeeping to
$10^{-9}$\,J (§\ref{sec:numerics}).

\subsection{What the can's $U$ does}
$U$ is the least certain number in the model. Time to target scales as $1/U$ \eqref{eq:scales}; the
\FLOOR\ results do not depend on it at all. The SINTEF benchmark it came from cannot also be used to
validate it. A $U$ fitted against a probe out in the bath includes the spreading of heat through the
bath; against a probe at the can wall it does not.

\section{Notes for whoever picks this up}

\textbf{Do first.} E1. It costs one afternoon and decides between $-17.8$ and $-21.2\dC$ for
every later run, and whether the bath in E3 is a thermostat or a drifting reservoir.

\textbf{Do not} quote the rig-note charge as buffered; do not invert a blank run with $L_f$;
do not report a single latent-heat share without the starting temperature of the water; and do not
fit $U$ against a bulk probe and then use it with heat spreading switched on.

\textbf{The sharpest predictions}, cheapest first: the rig-note charge settles near $-17.8\dC$
with no crystals left; the original \code{sypt code} charge leaves only about 50\,g of ice;
the buffered bath holds $-21.2\dC$ for about 2.5\,h on the leak alone; the brine at the can runs
6.5\,K above the bulk; a stalled one-shot bath is colder at the floor than in the bulk.

\textbf{Software.} \code{b8\_sim.py}: Python 3 with NumPy and SciPy, Matplotlib for the figures;
everything runs in about 12\,s. The simulator (\code{b8\_model.py}) needs only Python 3.11 or later,
plus Matplotlib for its pictures; one run takes about 0.5\,s.
